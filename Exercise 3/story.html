Story 1 · For everyday TV shoppers

Bigger screen, bigger bill.

This story is for someone standing in front of a wall of TVs, deciding what size to bring home. They don't want a spreadsheet — they want to know whether "bigger" quietly costs more, and by how much.

The big idea

75"+ screens are now the single most common size on the register — and that "default" choice costs roughly twice as much to run per year as a mid-size set.

01

Storyboard

ISSUE

Buyers still think of 55" as "big" — but oversized screens have quietly become the market default, and nobody connects that shift to their power bill.

02

How common is each screen size?

Across the 4,522 models registered for sale in Australia, the single most common size bracket is no longer the mid-range — it's 75" and up, at 27% of the market. The traditional "mid-size" bracket (43"–54") has slipped to third place.

Does a bigger screen really use more power?

Yes — and the climb is steep, not gentle. Average labelled annual energy use rises from around 93 kWh for the smallest sets to 768 kWh for 75"+ models, an increase that tracks screen size closely (screen size and annual energy use move together with a correlation of 0.86 out of 1.0).

Before / after

Defaulting to today's most common option — a 75"+ set — costs roughly $231 a year to run at $0.30/kWh. Sizing down to a set that fits a typical living room (55"–64") costs about $115 a year — you keep a genuinely large screen and still cut the running cost almost in half.

04

Before you buy

  • 1Measure your room and viewing distance before you shop — most buyers can comfortably size down from what looks impressive in a showroom.
  • 2Treat the biggest screen on the display wall as the shop's default, not yours — 75"+ is common precisely because it's what's pushed hardest.
  • 3Every size bracket up the scale adds real, ongoing cost — budget for the power bill, not just the sticker price.

Story 2 · For spec- and eco-conscious buyers

Does panel technology change the bill?

This story is for someone choosing between LCD, LED-LCD and OLED and wondering whether the premium panel comes with a hidden running-cost penalty — or whether that reputation is really about something else.

The big idea

Panel technology looks like a big energy factor until you control for screen size — then OLED's reputation as the power-hungry option mostly disappears, and in the large-screen class it's actually the most efficient of the three.

01

Storyboard

ISSUE

OLED's premium price gives it a reputation as an energy hog — shoppers may downgrade to LCD purely on energy-anxiety grounds.

DEMONSTRATE ISSUE

On the surface it checks out: OLEDs average 489 kWh/yr, plain LCDs only 335 kWh/yr — OLED looks about 46% thirstier.

DIG DEEPER

But LED-LCD dominates supply (81% of the market) and OLED sets registered for sale run far bigger on average (65" vs 51") — size, not the panel, may be doing the work.

EXPLAIN THE FACTOR

To compare fairly, group models into the same size class (small / medium / large) before comparing energy use across panel types.

BEFORE / AFTER

Raw comparison says OLED costs the most. Size-matched comparison says otherwise: in the large-screen class, OLED uses less than LED-LCD.

RECOMMENDATION

Choose panel technology for picture quality and budget — at a given size, OLED isn't the power hog its reputation suggests.

Dig deeper: what's actually on the shelf?

Two things complicate the raw comparison above. First, panel types aren't equally common — LED-LCD makes up the overwhelming majority of the market, with OLED still a small minority. Second, OLED models registered for sale tend to be considerably bigger sets than LCD ones — and Story 1 already showed that size alone drives most of the difference in running cost.

A fairer comparison: match on size first

Rather than lumping every screen size together, models were grouped into the same three size classes used in our size-frequency analysis — small (under 43"), medium (43"–65") and large (over 65") — before comparing panel types within each class. That's the same size-matching approach explored during data exploration, applied here to test whether the raw OLED-vs-LCD gap survives.

The twist

Once screen size is held constant, panel technology stops being the story. In the medium class, all three panel types land within about 30 kWh/yr of each other. In the large class — where most OLEDs actually sit — OLED (723 kWh/yr) uses less energy on average than LED-LCD (760 kWh/yr), and only the small-screen OLED group (n = 17) looks meaningfully higher, a result too thin to generalise from.

05

Choosing between panel types

  • 1Don't rule out OLED on energy grounds alone — at the same screen size, its running cost is close to LED-LCD's, and can be lower on large screens.
  • 2Pick panel technology for picture quality, viewing angle and budget — those are genuine, size-independent differences.
  • 3If cutting running cost is the real goal, screen size is still the lever that matters most — see Story 1.
