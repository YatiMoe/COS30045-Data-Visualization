Data story · Australian TV energy register

Bigger screen, bigger bill.

Before you buy, it helps to know what you're signing up for. We pulled 4,724 television models currently registered for sale in Australia and asked three questions a shopper standing in front of a wall of TVs would actually want answered: how common is the size you're eyeing, how much more does a bigger screen cost to run, and does the panel technology change that story?

The big idea

Screen size — not brand, not panel technology — is what really drives a television's running cost, and stepping up just one size bracket can add well over $100 a year to the power bill.

Who this is for

How common is each screen size?

Across the 4,724 models registered for sale, the market clusters firmly around the middle. The 43″–54″ bracket alone accounts for just over a quarter of every model on the register, more than double the share of anything under 32″.

Does a bigger screen really use more power?

Yes — and the climb is steep, not gentle. Average labelled annual energy use rises from around 104 kWh for the smallest sets to 879 kWh for 75″+ models, an increase that tracks screen size closely (screen size and annual energy use move together with a correlation of 0.86 out of 1.0).

What this means

Moving from the most popular bracket (43″–54″, ~337 kWh/year) up to a 75″+ set (~879 kWh/year) roughly 2.6×'s the annual energy bill for that appliance — at $0.30/kWh, that's the difference between roughly $101 and $264 a year, before the TV has done anything more than sit on the wall.

Does panel technology change the story?

Not as much as you'd expect. OLEDs do use more power on average than LED-LCDs, which use more than plain LCDs — but almost all of that gap disappears once screen size is accounted for.

The twist

OLEDs look like the biggest power users in the raw chart (486 kWh/year on average) — but that's mostly because OLED models registered for sale skew larger (64.5″ on average, versus 50.7″ for plain LCD). Once normalised per inch of screen, OLED (7.5 kWh/inch) and LED-LCD (7.7 kWh/inch) land in the same range, with older plain LCD panels actually the most efficient per inch (6.6 kWh/inch). Panel technology is a minor factor here — screen size is doing almost all of the work.

05

Before you buy

None of this means "buy the smallest TV you can find" — it means going in with your eyes open about what size actually costs to run, so the decision matches your room and your power bill, not just the display wall in-store.

  • 1Measure your room and viewing distance before you shop — most buyers can comfortably size down from what looks impressive in a showroom.
  • 2Treat panel technology (LCD vs LED-LCD vs OLED) as a picture-quality decision, not an energy decision — the running-cost difference between panels is small once size is equal.
  • 3Every size bracket up the scale adds real, ongoing cost — budget for the power bill, not just the sticker price.
  • 4Use the energy calculator on the Televisions page to estimate the actual yearly cost for the exact model and usage pattern you're considering.
